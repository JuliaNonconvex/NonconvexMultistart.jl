Deflation

Deflation is a technique for exploring multiple distinct local minimizers of a non-convex problem. Instead of restarting the optimization from random starting points and hoping for different solutions, deflation solves the problem repeatedly while adding a constraint that makes all previously found solutions infeasible, so every converged sub-problem is guaranteed to find a different solution.

DeflatedAlg and DeflationMultistartAlg implement the general deflation constraint of Tarek and Huang (2022). The key idea of the paper is that deflation can be expressed as a simple change of the problem formulation — a non-convex inequality constraint — rather than a change of the optimization algorithm, so any constraint-capable optimizer of the Nonconvex ecosystem can be used as the sub-solver.

Theory

This section summarizes the deflation constraint method implemented by this package, following Tarek and Huang (2022).

The problem

Consider the nonlinear program

\[\begin{aligned} \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^n} \; & f(x) \\ \text{s.t.} \quad & c(x) = 0, \\ & l \le x \le u, \end{aligned}\]

where the objective $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ and the equality constraints $c : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ ($m \le n$) are twice continuously differentiable and $l, u \in \mathbb{R}^n$ are finite lower and upper bounds. General nonlinear inequality constraints can be converted to equality constraints by adding slack variables.

A local minimizer $x^*$ satisfies (under a regularity condition) the first-order Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions

\[\nabla_x L(x^*, \lambda, z^+, z^-) = 0, \qquad c(x^*) = 0, \qquad l \le x^* \le u,\]

together with the dual-feasibility and complementarity conditions, where

\[L(x, \lambda, z^+, z^-) = f(x) + c(x)^\top \lambda + (x - u)^\top z^+ - (x - l)^\top z^-\]

is the Lagrangian and $\lambda$, $z^+$, $z^-$ are the multipliers of the equality and bound constraints. Local first- and second-order optimization algorithms converge to whichever KKT point lies in the basin of attraction of the initial guess, and naive multi-start strategies can waste many restarts re-converging to the same solution.

Classical deflation for nonlinear systems

Deflation originates from nonlinear root finding (Farrell, Beentjes and Birkisson, 2016). After a root $x_1$ of a nonlinear system $F(x) = 0$ has been found, the deflation operator

\[M(x; x_1) = m(x; x_1) \, I, \qquad m(x; x_1) = \lVert x - x_1 \rVert^{-p} + \sigma,\]

for some power $p$ and shift $\sigma$ ($I$ the identity), is applied to obtain the deflated system $M(x; x_1) F(x) = 0$. Since $\lVert M(x; x_1) F(x) \rVert$ does not vanish as $x \to x_1$, a Newton-type method cannot reconverge to $x_1$ and is forced to find a different root. For several known roots, the operators multiply, $\prod_{k=1}^{\tilde{K}} M(x; x_k) F(x) = 0$ (summation can be used instead of multiplication for numerical stability).

Deflating the KKT conditions is intrusive

To apply deflation to optimization, one can deflate the optimality conditions themselves: write (most of) the KKT conditions as a nonlinear system $F(x, \lambda) = 0$ and solve the deflated system $M(x; x_1) F(x, \lambda) = 0$ while taking descent steps. However, this requires a Newton-like solver on the deflated KKT system, whose Jacobian is no longer symmetric — the top-left block is the Hessian of the Lagrangian scaled by the deflation operator, while off-the-shelf NLP solvers such as IPOPT assume it to be symmetric. This is why Papadopoulos, Farrell and Surowiec (2021) had to implement a specialized primal-dual interior-point method from scratch. The constraint formulation below avoids this entirely: it changes the problem, not the algorithm.

Deflation as a constraint (Lemma 4.1)

Introduce an auxiliary variable $y \ge 0$ and add the deflation constraint $m(x; x_1) \le y$ to the NLP:

\[\min_{x, y} \; f(x) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad c(x) = 0, \quad m(x; x_1) \le y, \quad l \le x \le u.\]

Lemma 4.1 (Tarek and Huang). If $(x^*, y^*)$ is a regular KKT point of this problem with finite $y^*$, and $m$ is bounded from below, then $x^*$ is a regular KKT point of the original NLP and $x^* \neq x_1$.

Sketch of proof. Let $\eta \ge 0$ be the multiplier of the deflation constraint $m(x; x_1) - y \le 0$. Differentiating the Lagrangian with respect to $y$ gives $-\eta$, so stationarity forces $\eta = 0$, and the stationarity conditions of the deflated problem reduce to those of the original NLP. Since the constraints of the original problem are a subset of the deflated ones, $x^*$ is feasible and the complementarity conditions carry over. Finally, because $m(x; x_1) \to \infty$ as $x \to x_1$ while $m(x^*) \le y^* < \infty$, it follows that $x^* \neq x_1$. The same argument extends to inequality- and conic-constrained NLPs.

For several known solutions $x_1, \dots, x_{\tilde{K}}$, the constraint becomes the sum

\[\sum_{k=1}^{\tilde{K}} m(x; x_k) \le y .\]

This is exactly the constraint added by DeflatedAlg after each found solution, with $m(x; x_k) = \max(d(x, x_k) - r, 0)^{-p} + \sigma$ and $y$ bounded above by y_upper (the finite upper bound guarantees that $y^*$ is finite).

The fixed-bound formulation (Lemma 4.2)

Alternatively, the auxiliary variable can be replaced by a large finite constant $M$:

\[\min_x \; f(x) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad c(x) = 0, \quad m(x; x_1) \le M, \quad l \le x \le u.\]

Lemma 4.2 (Tarek and Huang). If $x^*$ is a regular KKT point at which the deflation constraint holds at a strict inequality, and $m$ is bounded from below, then $x^*$ is a regular KKT point of the original NLP and $x^* \neq x_1$.

Sketch of proof. By complementarity slackness, strict feasibility of the deflation constraint forces its multiplier $\eta = 0$; the rest of the proof is as in Lemma 4.1.

This is the formulation used by DeflationMultistartAlg with $M$ = y_upper: because no auxiliary variable is added, the multistart sampler explores only the original variables.

Deflation is a minimum-distance constraint

The deflation constraint is equivalent to a minimum-distance constraint (Section 4.5 of the paper): with finite, positive $m$ and $y > \sigma$,

\[m(x; x_1) = \lVert x - x_1 \rVert^{-p} + \sigma \le y \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \lVert x - x_1 \rVert^{p} \ge z, \quad z = \frac{1}{y - \sigma}.\]

Deflation therefore simply forbids a neighbourhood of every known solution. If the solver drives $y \to \infty$ — or, in the fixed-bound formulation, if the deflation constraint is satisfied at equality — deflation has failed to push the solution away from the known ones.

Distance measures

The distance $d(x, x_k)$ in the deflation function can be adapted to the problem (Section 4.6 of the paper):

  • a power $p$ of an $\ell_q$-norm distance instead of a power of the Euclidean norm;
  • a positive semi-definite weight matrix $Q$, giving the generalized Euclidean distance $\sqrt{(x - x_k)^\top Q (x - x_k)}$; a diagonal $Q$ weighs the deviation terms per coordinate (the weight option);
  • a problem-specific distance, e.g. in a transformed space such as density-filtered designs in topology optimization, the Kullback–Leibler divergence between variational distributions, or neuron activations instead of weights (the distance option);
  • a radius $r$, giving $m(x; x_k) = \max(\lVert x - x_k \rVert - r, 0)^{-p}$: all points within distance $r$ of a known solution are treated as identical, so even if deflation fails to find a new minimizer, the returned solution is guaranteed to lie outside the $r$-ball. This is useful in high dimensions, where two solutions can be numerically different but practically identical (the radius option).

Failure modes and how the implementation detects them

Deflation is not a global optimization algorithm, and the following caveats (Section 4.7 of the paper) apply. The implementation guards against the first two:

  1. The deflation effect may be too weak. The solver can still approach $x_1$ asymptotically with $y \to \infty$ (or with the constraint active at $M$). By the minimum-distance equivalence above, the constraint excludes a hyper-ball of radius $(y_{\mathrm{upper}} - \tilde{K} \sigma)^{-1/p}$ around each known solution. Every new solution is therefore validated against this exclusion radius: a solution closer than $\max(\mathrm{tol}, r + 100 \cdot (y_{\mathrm{upper}} - \tilde{K} \sigma)^{-1/p})$ to a known solution is rejected as a deflation failure and the run stops early with converged = false.
  2. Optimization happens in finite precision. A failed deflation converges to a point with a large-but-finite constraint value rather than an infinite one, so the finiteness of $y$ alone is not a reliable distinctness test; the distance-based test above is used instead.
  3. Convergence to a new minimizer is not guaranteed. Deflation guarantees distinctness if the sub-problem converges, but a local solver started from the same initial point may fail to escape to a new basin. Deflating one solution can also push the optimizer away from other nearby local minima.
  4. Hyperparameter sensitivity. The power $p$ and the other hyperparameters of the distance measure must be tuned for the problem at hand.

Construct an instance

To construct a deflation meta-algorithm wrapping Ipopt, use:

using NonconvexMultistart, NonconvexIpopt

alg = DeflatedAlg(IpoptAlg())
options = DeflationOptions(sub_options = IpoptOptions(), iters = 10)
result = optimize(model, alg, x0, options = options)

DeflatedAlg can wrap any optimizer of the Nonconvex ecosystem that supports nonlinear inequality constraints, e.g. IpoptAlg(), MMA87(), MMA02() or AugLagAlg().

Combining deflation with multistart

Plain deflation re-solves every sub-problem from the same initial point, so a local sub-solver may repeatedly converge to the boundary of the exclusion region around a known solution instead of a new minimizer. DeflationMultistartAlg combines deflation with an existing multistart meta-algorithm: every deflation round runs a full multistart search of the deflated sub-problem, so the starting point is explored by the sampler while the deflation constraints keep the solutions distinct.

alg = DeflationMultistartAlg(HyperoptAlg(IpoptAlg()))
options = DeflationMultistartOptions(
    multi_options = HyperoptOptions(sub_options = IpoptOptions(), iters = 10),
    iters = 4,
)
result = optimize(model, alg, x0, options = options)

Here options.iters is the maximum number of distinct minimizers to find (one deflation round per solution), while multi_options.iters is the number of candidate starting points evaluated by the multistart sampler in each round. The fixed-bound formulation (Lemma 4.2 above) is used for the deflation constraint, so no auxiliary variable is added and the multistart sampler explores only the original variables.

Options

All the options that can be passed to the DeflationOptions constructor:

  • sub_options: the options of the sub-optimizer, or a function from the deflation iteration index to the sub-optimizer options.
  • iters: the maximum number of distinct minimizers to find, 10 by default.
  • p: the deflation power in d(x, x_j)⁻ᵖ, 2.0 by default.
  • sigma: the shift σ added to every deflation term, 0.0 by default.
  • radius: the radius r of the hyper-ball around each known solution inside which all points are deflated identically, 0.0 by default. With radius > 0, every new solution is guaranteed to be at least radius away from all previously found ones, even if deflation fails. This is particularly useful in high dimensions, where two solutions can be numerically different but practically identical.
  • distance: an optional custom distance function d(x, x_j), e.g. to deflate in a transformed space (density-filtered designs, KL divergence between variational distributions, neuron values instead of weights, …).
  • weight: an optional positive semi-definite matrix Q giving the generalized Euclidean distance sqrt((x - x_j)' Q (x - x_j)). A diagonal Q weighs the deviation terms per coordinate. Only one of distance and weight can be set.
  • y_upper: the finite upper bound on the deflation variable y, 1e8 by default. A larger y_upper allows solutions closer to the known ones to be considered distinct.
  • ctol: the infeasibility tolerance for accepting a sub-problem solution as feasible, 1e-5 by default.
  • tol: the distance tolerance used to decide that a new solution is identical to a previously found one, 1e-6 by default.
  • keep_all: when true (the default), all the sub-problem results are kept in the results field of the returned result; when false, only the found minimizers are returned.

DeflationMultistartOptions has the same deflation hyperparameters plus multi_options, the options of the wrapped multistart algorithm; in that variant y_upper is the fixed bound M of the deflation constraint.

Result

The result of optimize with a DeflatedAlg or a DeflationMultistartAlg is a DeflationResult with the following fields:

  • minimum: the best objective value among the found minimizers.
  • minimizer: the found minimizer with the best objective value.
  • minimizers: all the distinct feasible minimizers found, in the order they were found.
  • results: all the sub-problem results when keep_all = true, nothing otherwise.
  • converged: true if all iters deflation iterations produced feasible solutions distinct from all previously found ones.
  • niters: the number of sub-problems actually solved before stopping.

For example:

r = optimize(model, alg, x0, options = options)
r.minimum
r.minimizer
r.minimizers
r.converged
r.niters

Limitations

Deflation guarantees that if a sub-problem converges, its solution is different from all previously found ones; it does not guarantee that convergence to a new minimizer will happen. In particular, a local sub-solver started from the same initial point every time may simply converge to the boundary of the exclusion region around a known solution, in which case the run stops early and converged is false. Combining deflation with multistart (DeflationMultistartAlg) mitigates this, since every deflation round explores multiple starting points. Tuning p, radius, y_upper and the distance measure, or starting the deflation iterations from different initial points, can also help in practice.
